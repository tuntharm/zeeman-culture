Translation is only the beginning

A message can be accurate in two languages and still feel different in each. Tone, context and cultural relevance shape what happens next.

Sample editorial
Sample editorial

An illustrative article for this journal. It does not describe a Zeeman client campaign.

A sentence can cross a border more easily than the idea behind it. The words may be correct, yet the invitation can feel distant, the humour can fall flat, or the reason to care can remain unclear. For a brand working between London and Shanghai, translation is a useful starting point. The next task is to understand how that message meets a person's everyday life.

Imagine an independent food brand introducing a product to a new audience. Its original campaign centres on a childhood memory. A literal translation preserves the description, but readers who do not share that memory may miss the emotional connection. The creative question becomes more interesting: which familiar moment could give this audience a way into the same feeling, without inventing a new identity for the brand?

Start with the situation

Before rewriting a headline, ask where someone will encounter it. Are they scrolling between appointments, searching for a place to meet friends, or comparing products before a purchase? A useful message answers the question already in their mind. Language is part of that answer, alongside imagery, timing, format and the details that help a person decide whether the offer belongs in their life.

Geography alone cannot provide this context. Chinese-speaking audiences in the UK are not one group, just as an audience in Shanghai does not share a single taste. A student, a long-term resident and a visiting professional may approach the same product differently. The brief should name the audience and situation clearly enough for a local collaborator to challenge its assumptions.

Keep the intention, reconsider the expression

Separate the brand's essential promise from the creative choices used to express it. Product facts, commitments and the intended feeling need consistency. The opening line, visual reference or creator's anecdote may need flexibility. This gives a translator, strategist and creator something concrete to work towards together, rather than asking each to guess which parts of the original campaign are negotiable.

A practical review can begin with two versions of the same idea: one close to the original language and another shaped around the new audience's context. Ask readers what each version means to them, what feels unclear and what they would expect next. Their explanations are more useful at this stage than a simple preference between two polished headlines.

Make room for a second reading

Review the whole encounter, including the image, caption, landing page and reply to a question. A thoughtful headline cannot carry a confusing journey by itself. Let someone familiar with the audience explain the message back in their own words, and listen for a gap between the brand's intention and their interpretation.

That gap is where cross-cultural creative work becomes valuable. The aim is a recognisable brand meeting a specific audience with attention and respect. A good final question is simple: beyond understanding these words, does someone have a reason to continue the conversation?
